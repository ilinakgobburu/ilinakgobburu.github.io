A survivorship-bias-free S&P 500 panel, point in time since 1996

Every firm in the S&P 500 on each date since 1996, including the ones later removed, merged or delisted, with a permanent SEC ID (CIK) for firms from 2016 on, so renamed and reused tickers don't split one company or splice two together. On top sits a daily volatility layer built from intraday trades on 3,241 trading days, 2014–2026. Backtesting on today's member list overstates an equal-weighted S&P 500 by 5.1 points a year, and volatility forecasts tested only on today's members look better than they are.

Why it is needed: free index lists only know the survivors

  • Today's list hides the losers. Free S&P 500 lists give only today's members. A backtest built on them quietly drops every firm that failed or was acquired, so the strategy looks better than it was.
  • Tickers are not firms. CCI was Citicorp in 1996 and is Crown Castle today; FB became META. A join on ticker either splices two companies together or loses one.
  • The free volatility source closed. The standard free library of daily realized volatility closed in 2022, and the closest current one covers only today's large caps, so it has the same survivor problem.

  • Today's list holds only the winners, firms that grew into the index, so the bias is largest when every stock gets the same weight.
  • It hits volatile stocks hardest. Today's list adds 12.1 points a year to the most volatile fifth of stocks and 0.3 to the least volatile. Standard factor exposures explain only 0.4 of the 12.1 points.
  • Leaving is not failing. Of the 116 firms delisted by 2025, 106 were acquired and 10 failed.

Forecasting volatility on the true index

The project asked whether Wikipedia attention or a firm's place in the correlation network helps forecast its volatility. Each group of signals was added in turn to a standard HAR model (past daily, weekly and monthly volatility), refit every month on all members and scored only on later months, 2016–2026.

Lower forecast error than HAR alone (QLIKE loss), so higher is better. Models with every signal are scored through August 2025, where the options data ends.

  • Attention and network add nothing. Neither improved on HAR, alone or on top of options-implied volatility. The useful information was in option prices and the earnings calendar.
  • HAR is hard to beat. It already cuts forecast error by 18% a day ahead and 21% a week ahead against carrying recent volatility forward.
  • The combined model held up in stress. LASSO with every signal beat HAR in calm periods, the 2022 rate selloff and the 2023 bank failures. In the COVID crash it helped a day ahead but not a week ahead. Gradient boosting with the same signals broke down in COVID because it can't forecast outside the range it trained on.
  • Survivorship shows up here too. 233 of the 763 stocks in the index since 2016 have left it. They had 34% more variance and were 15% (next day) to 24% (next week) harder to forecast, so testing only on today's members makes HAR look 2.5% to 3.8% better than it is. Most of that comes from takeovers. Acquired firms were no more volatile than the rest but much harder to forecast.
  • Earnings days matter. They are 3.2% of stock-days but carry 8.2% of all variance.

Two case studies

For an investor: a volatility-targeted portfolio
  • The setup. Equal weights, a 15% volatility target, rebalanced weekly, 2016–2025, run on the true index and on today's list.
  • Today's list flatters it. With the HAR forecast it returns 12.9% a year against 9.8% on the true index (+3.1 points, 95% interval 2.0 to 4.4). $1 million grows to $3.05 million instead of $2.36 million.
  • Most of the gap isn't delistings. About 2.3 to 2.6 of the 3.1 points come from firms that left the index but still trade, mostly removed for size. Dropping only delisted firms adds 0.5 points.
  • The forecast mattered for risk, not return. No forecast changed the return detectably. HAR forecast the portfolio's risk better than the naive forecast did.
For a researcher: rerunning a published comparison
  • The paper. Brini (2026) compares HAR models with a time-series foundation model (TTM) on 40 stocks that survived the whole sample.
  • Reproduced first. The paper's own code, run unchanged on its own data, matches its published results to within 0.0024 (QLIKE).
  • Then rerun on three lists: the paper's 40 stocks, the 40 largest each year picked without hindsight, and every S&P 500 member on each date (611 stocks).
  • The result holds, weakly. TTM's error is 1.4% to 3.1% lower than Log-HAR's, but only one result (every member, a week ahead) is clearly different from zero, and on the full index much of the edge comes from 2020. Survivorship doesn't drive it.

What is built

  • Index membership, 1996 to 2026. 1,272 index stays, all kept, including firms that left. It matches CRSP's licensed list on all but 92 of 1.27 million stock-days.
  • Permanent firm IDs from 2016 on for 713 of 767 firm segments, with the evidence for each ID, or the reason it was withheld, stored next to it.
  • Intraday trades for every member on 3,241 trading days, 2014–2026, pulled as batch jobs on the WRDS Cloud. From them come daily realized volatility (with its up and down parts and jumps) for 1.63 million stock-days, and a daily correlation network between firms.
  • Bad-print repair. One trade reported far from the market can fake a large move (UNH on 2016-03-17 showed 179 times an outside source's volatility). Suspect windows are pulled again and cleaned with a published filter. Nine stock-days the repair couldn't fix were excluded after a review.
  • Outside checks. Forty large caps track VOLARE's volatility with a correlation of 0.988 over 116,938 stock-days, and their covariances at 0.992. SPY's volatility tracks Oxford-Man's library at 0.976 over 2,041 days (2014 until it closed in 2022). TAQ closing prices match CRSP's on all 2,834 stock-days checked.
  • Forecasting signals. Wikipedia pageviews for 570 of 678 firms, FRED macro series, options-implied volatility and earnings dates.
  • Tools for users. A loader that returns the index on any date, and a checker that reports how far a backtest's ticker list is from the true index. There is also a fixed volatility forecasting benchmark and a Parquet and DuckDB export.
  • One documented SQLite database of 16 tables, rebuilt from the raw inputs in one step. Over 600 automated tests run on every pull request, on Linux and a Mac. In a planted-bug test they caught 41 of 43 deliberate bugs.

Firm IDs: an LLM proposes, two sources check

How one ID is decided
  • A local LLM names the company that traded under the ticker on its last day in the index. That is its only job.
  • SEC EDGAR supplies the ID. The CIK is the one EDGAR's records give for that name, never a number the model wrote.
  • Wikipedia is the second witness. Its record of index changes says which firm S&P removed that day. When it disagrees with the model, the model's answer is dropped and Wikipedia's name is searched instead.
  • Conflicts are withheld, not guessed. When the evidence disagrees, the ID stays empty and the reason is recorded.
Why the checks matter

The model was also asked for each CIK. None of its 275 was right, and it rated every one "high" confidence. It fits names to letters: TAP became Atmos Energy (it is Molson Coors).

An early version trusted an EDGAR name match alone. An audit found that 44 of its 144 IDs (31%) were the wrong firm, real companies whose names happen to fit the ticker. With the second source and rules for known traps, the final build finds 217 of the 275 IDs and withholds the rest.

Limits

  • Coverage. Realized volatility and the network start in 2014, every other layer in 2016. Firm IDs before 2016 are unresolved, and 54 segments from 2016 on still have no ID.
  • Older tickers. The free membership file uses today's tickers for some older companies. The three cases since 2016 (Tyco, the old Chubb and Arconic) are fixed; older ones are still folded in.
  • Earnings dates are treated as known in advance. Firms announce them weeks ahead, but a date that later moved is a small look-ahead.
  • The portfolio levels are loose. Nine years of weekly returns leave wide intervals on each portfolio's return. Only the gap between the true index and today's list is precise.
  • The hand check is small and was not blind. On a 30-stay random sample, 25 IDs agreed, none was wrong and 5 were blank. The answers were drafted by an AI assistant after seeing the pipeline's output and reviewed by a team member. With 30 rows, these are estimates.
  • Licensed data stays licensed. The membership, firm-ID and attention layers use only public sources. The intraday trades (TAQ), CRSP returns, options-implied volatility (OptionMetrics) and earnings dates (Compustat) are licensed through WRDS, so only aggregate results are shown.
