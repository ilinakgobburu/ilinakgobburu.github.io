The two-sided variance trade

Options price a stock's downside and its upside separately. This strategy sells whichever side is expensive against the stock's own realized moves on that side and buys the other. Shares hedge the direction away, so the profit should come from the gap between implied and realized volatility. Four decision rules, from always selling the put side to a random forest, were registered (written down and committed) before any of the strategy's quantities were computed on real quotes. The results are from development, plus one run on NVIDIA (NVDA) that is reported but not scored as a test. The final test has not been run.

How the trade works

  • Measure each side. The model-free variance sum behind the VIX, split at the at-the-money strike, gives the puts and the calls each their own implied variance. Each side's premium is the log ratio of that variance to the stock's down-day (or up-day) variance over the last 63 returns. It is taken net of the split a flat smile (the same implied vol at every strike) would give.
  • Trade. Sell the rich side's 0.25-delta option and buy the cheap side's, about $200 of vega each, so the pair starts with roughly no exposure to a parallel move in implied vol. Both legs are held to the next monthly expiry. One stock over one such cycle is a stock-cycle.
  • Hedge and fills. Shares offset the options' delta at every open, on a $100,000 book per stock. Results are shown at mid (halfway between bid and ask) and at a stress fill that crosses the bid/ask and pays 5 bp on every hedge trade.

The model ladder and how it was tested

  • Four rungs. Rule 0 always sells the put side, as option sellers habitually do, and is the bar. Rule 1 sells whichever side's premium is higher. A logistic regression and a shallow random forest (500 trees of depth 3) predict whether selling the put side will make money. Their nine inputs are known at entry and include the two side premiums, the smile's tilt and the VIX.
  • Stay out when unsure. A model sells the put side when its probability is above 0.55, the call side when it is below 0.45, and does nothing in between.
  • Walk-forward. Each month the models are refit only on stock-cycles that had already expired, from 77 training rows in January 2021 to 200 in November 2023.
  • One rung for the final test. A more complex rung goes to the final test (six stocks, 2024–26, allowed a single run) only if its development Sharpe ratio at mid beats the simpler candidates'. Rule 0 is the pass bar, not a candidate.
  • Written down first. Every rule, down to the pass bar, was committed on November 11 and 15, 2026, before any of this strategy's implied variances, features or P&L were computed on real quotes. Two amendments that net the flat-smile split out of the signal were made after tests on synthetic data, before any run on real data. The rules were written knowing an SPY pilot of the measures and the wheel's results on these stocks in these years. Neither computed this strategy's quantities.

Where the P&L came from

Each trade's daily P&L was split with each leg's greeks into stock moves (gamma), time decay (theta) and implied-vol changes (vega). The hedge's P&L and a residual are kept separate. The split is reported only. Dollars are summed over the five stocks and the 36 scored months.

  • The hedged put alone about broke even before costs. Its time decay (+⁠$606,249) was more than used up by stock moves (−⁠$540,821) and implied-vol changes (−⁠$109,257). With a residual of +⁠$45,606 and −⁠$569 from the delta hedge, it ended at +⁠$1,209 at mid and −⁠$32,126 after costs.
  • Costs were far larger than the edge. Always selling the put side made $2,724 at mid. The spread and hedge costs at the stress fill were $56,401, which left a loss of $53,677.
  • The signal did not pick the winning side. Rule 1, which trades the side premiums directly, did worse than always selling the put side (Sharpe ratio −0.47 against +0.04 at mid). Hit rates fell from 55% for rule 0 to 37% for the forest.
  • The forest was rarely confident. Its probabilities ran from 0.41 to 0.63 and fell inside the no-trade band for 93 of 128 stock-cycles.

NVDA alone, 2024–26

Reported, not judged. This run was added on November 17, after the development run. Its registration and script were committed before anything was computed. The same rules ran on NVDA alone with one $100,000 book, over 33 monthly cycles from January 2024 to October 2026 (29 measured). For this run, no other test stock's 2024–26 data was read.

Two things limit what it shows. NVDA is one of the six final-test stocks, so these are its test-window months. NVDA is also one of the five development stocks, and the only one where the logistic regression's development Sharpe ratio at mid was above zero (+0.40). Limits

  • Development only. No final-test result exists. The five stocks share 36 calendar months, so one bad month for the market moves every book at once.
  • Closing quotes. Options fill at the entry close, since the data holds closing quotes only. Live fills come at the next open, and the stress fill cannot show overnight moves.
  • A corridor of strikes. Each side's variance uses only strikes within 1.5 standard deviations of the forward, so it leaves out the far tails. Of 295 development stock-cycles, 205 were measured and the rest skipped for thin quotes (Disney was measured in only 22 of 59).
  • Earnings and exercise. The earnings input uses actual report dates, while a live trader knows only the expected ones. Early exercise and assignment are not modeled.
