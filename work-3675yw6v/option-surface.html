Where listed option prices actually exist

A listed option chain looks like a full grid of prices. It isn't: many contracts carry a mark, the midpoint of the closing bid and ask, with no trade behind it, and the mark itself is not a price anyone could trade at. This project measures both problems on options on UUUU, a thinly traded small-cap, with CCJ, a far larger and more liquid name, as a control.

How it was tested

  • Mark against last trade. For every listed contract on every session: is there a mark, a trade, both or neither, and how far apart are they?
  • Model-free no-arbitrage checks. Strike monotonicity, butterfly convexity and the intrinsic-value floor, tested at the mid and again at prices you could actually trade at.
  • Leave-one-out interpolation test. Hide a known price, rebuild it from its neighbors and measure the miss, once in price and once in implied volatility (Black-76).
  • Put-call parity fit. The forward and the discount factor are backed out of the option prices themselves, with no rate or dividend assumed.
  • The same pipeline on a control. CCJ runs through identical code.

Does it generalize? CCJ as a control

The obvious guess was that a more liquid name would close the holes. CCJ's quotes were tighter and more of its trades printed near the mid, yet a larger share of its listed contracts never traded. A bigger name lists more contracts (17,324 against 11,114), and more of them end up marked but never traded.

UUUU against CCJall sessions pooled
UUUU against CCJ. Listed series with a mark but no trade: 30.5% vs 46.1%. Trades printed near the mid: 29.3% vs 33.4%. Median bid-ask spread: 20.3% vs 18.7% of the mark. Median gap between mark and trade: 4.4% vs 5.2% of the mark.

Limits

  • Executable prices are not perfectly clean. 16 of 4,147 put-call parity conversions survived paying the spread on all four legs. Early exercise and borrowing costs on these American options were not modeled.
  • A suggestive control, not a matched one. The two names differ in strike spacing and price level, so the direction is informative but the magnitudes are not strictly comparable.
  • Sparsity is a lower bound. A contract listed with neither a quote nor a trade leaves no trace in the data, so the real holes are at least this large.
