A weekly covered call, and which decisions mattered

Hold 100 Apple shares, sell one call that expires at the end of the week, hold it to expiry, and repeat. With the strategy fixed, the project measures how much three implementation choices move the result: the strike, the fill price and the time of the order. The backtest covers 10 weekly cycles in summer 2026 on hourly option data from LSEG.

How it was tested

  • Rules fixed before testing. Every parameter was set before any result was seen; none was chosen afterward.
  • Event-driven backtest. Every trade is booked, and the account is marked at each close from that trade record, so the two cannot disagree.
  • Reg T margin accounting, including interest on borrowed cash. Available funds never went negative, so every trade was fundable when booked.
  • Fill check. The mid was tested against actual trade prices by price band, moneyness and liquidity, and validated against a one-minute re-pull of the same contracts.
  • Sensitivity runs on the same engine: every order time in the session, and alternative strike rules, two of them set from implied volatility (Black-76).

Limits

  • It did not beat buy-and-hold. Apple rose 13.7% from the first purchase, so on the 8 assigned weeks the cap gave up $7,306 of upside for $2,572 of premium. None of the six strike rules beat holding the shares.
  • One price path. Ten weekly cycles on one stock in one quarter, with no substantial drawdown in the window, are closer to one observation than ten. The ranking of rules may not hold in another quarter.
  • The mid fill holds only at small size. The mid tracked trade prices closely, but only 27.4% of trades printed within a quarter-spread of it: fine for one contract a week on a liquid chain, not for larger orders.
