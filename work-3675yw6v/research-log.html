Research log: ideas tested and rejected

About two dozen trading ideas tested in October and November 2026. For each, the question, the rules and a pass bar were committed before any data was pulled; it was run once, and the result was recorded as it came out. Most failed. They're listed here with their bars, because a list of only the winners would hide how they were found.

How the tests were kept honest

  • Pass bars fixed in advance, with any later change dated and explained.
  • One-shot final tests, and gates that stop a weak idea before it can spend one.
  • Chance baselines: random stocks, permutation and rotation tests.
  • Mid and stress fills for every trading result.
  • Independent reviews and tested code before each run; after-the-fact results are labeled as such.

Option selling: the wheel

  • Timing the wheel with RSI. Skip puts when the stock looked stretched, or calls when it looked weak. Bar: beat the plain wheel in 7 of 9 windows. Result: 2, 0 and 2 of 9; stretched stocks kept going.
  • Pairs-traded wheels, four versions. Bar: beat both plain wheels in 4 of 5 years. Result: 0 of 5 each; in one version a short leg ran away and was margin-called.
  • Sell only when implied beats realized vol. Bar: 5 of 9 windows, deliberately weak. Result: passed on its second, adjusted version, then held 5 of 9 (6 at stress fills) on the test windows, mostly by sitting out the 2022 sell-off. It skipped nearly half the puts. A weak pass, not adopted.
  • The pair of wheels whose losses coincided least. Result: it ranked below the median pair afterward, and the ranking didn't persist (p 0.30 and 0.21).
  • A barbell: wheel the mean-reverting stock, hold the momentum stock. Bar: beat the alternatives in 6 of 8 folds. Result: 3, 2 and 5 of 8.
  • Practitioner management: early closes, rolls and a trend filter. Result: beat the plain wheel's Sharpe ratio in only 7 and 8 of 18 stock-windows.
  • Selling puts when market-wide option premium is high. Result: better on 4 of 5 stocks, until a rotation test gave p 0.31: one crash entry carried the whole difference.
  • A jet-fuel hedge for an airline. Result: Delta and fuel moved together, so the hedge had nothing to offset; it never beat the plain wheel.

The Poor Man's Covered Call

  • Seasonality screens for the stock choice. Two screens (Apple, then Delta) passed their rules (p 0.023 and 0.020), but every peak month contained earnings and the ranking didn't persist: an earnings effect, not seasonality.
  • The best in-sample option setting. Result: it lost to the baseline on all four cost bases in both out-of-sample samples.
  • Hedging the direction out. Result: 0 of 3 screened stocks beat T-bills on all four cost bases. Full write-up →

Volatility and machine learning

  • Can ML pick the most overpriced options? The pre-chosen logistic regression trailed a one-line ratio rule by 0.42 vol points and failed its bar; the simple rule held up on held-out years (t 4.1). The one signal here that survived. Full write-up →
  • Can you time selling single-stock volatility? "Options look rich against last month" had no relation to the next month's premium over 120 months (r = −0.01). Full write-up →
  • A screen for options that look too rich. The top of the list was special situations (pending deals, new listings, earnings timing), not mispricing.

Momentum, reversal and data

  • Switching between reversal and momentum, three published signals. Bar: t ≥ 1 and a Sharpe ratio above a fixed blend. Result: the best had t 0.52, and its whole edge was one month, April 2020, so the final-test window was left unused.
  • Data traps caught before any backtest: a stock split that showed a false 74% loss on a long option, and missing bids returned in a type the code had to read as zero bids.
  • A survivor-only universe: a data check found the vendor has no implied vol for firms that left the index, so an ML panel built on it would hold only survivors.
