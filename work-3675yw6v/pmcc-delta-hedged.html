Hedging the direction out of a PMCC: a premium too small to pay its spreads

A follow-up to the Poor Man's Covered Call on Delta Air Lines, prompted by a question in the course. Trade the stock every day to hold the position's delta at zero, so the only income left is the short call's implied volatility beating the volatility the stock then delivers. Does that premium beat T-bills? Tested on five stocks and two windows, each test registered before it ran.

Return a year above 3-month T-bills, compounded, on the cash the margin rules required at the tightest point. Fills at mid or at bid/ask, each with and without interest on short-sale proceeds (a retail account usually doesn't get it). The pass bar was registered for the screen's pick, Disney; applying it to the other samples is after the fact.

What it found

  • One source of income. With direction hedged away, the book earns only the short call's volatility premium, and the long-dated call pays for the same richness the short call sells.
  • Past premium doesn't pick stocks. Disney, the screen's top pick, went from an 8.0% premium in 2010–18 to −0.3% in 2019–23. Even where the premium held, spreads ate it.
  • No tweak helped. The best setting found in-sample lost to the baseline on all four cost bases in both out-of-sample samples. Volatility filters and protective legs didn't help either.
  • No free lunch in the synthetic stock. Put-call parity held; adding a synthetic future cancels the volatility income.

Limits

  • Single stocks at retail spreads. Index options, with tighter spreads, weren't tested here.
  • Few samples. Five stocks, and the only pass is the sample the setup was built on.
  • Daily closing quotes, one contract. The stock hedge pays $0.005 a share, but a per-order minimum isn't modeled (about $950 more over five years on Delta).
