A survivorship-bias-free S&P 500 panel, point in time since 1996

Every firm in the S&P 500 on each date since 1996, including the ones later removed, merged or delisted. Each firm is meant to sit under one permanent SEC ID, its CIK, so renamed and reused tickers don't split one company or splice two together (a few known mismatches are being fixed). Index membership and, from 2016 on, firm IDs are built; a daily volatility layer is being added on top.

Why it is needed: free index lists only know the survivors

  • Today's list hides the losers. Free S&P 500 lists give only today's members. A backtest built on them quietly drops every firm that failed or was acquired, so the strategy looks better than it was.
  • Tickers are not firms. CCI was Citicorp in 1996 and is Crown Castle today; FB became META. A join on ticker either splices two companies together or loses one.
  • The free volatility source closed. The standard free library of daily realized volatility closed in 2022, and the closest current one covers only today's large caps, so it has the same survivor problem.

What is built, and what is not yet

Built
  • Index membership, 1996 to 2026. Every stay kept, including firms that left; today's members match Wikipedia's current list.
  • Permanent firm IDs from 2016 on, with the evidence for each ID, or the reason it was withheld, stored next to it.
  • One documented SQLite database, rebuilt from the raw inputs in one step; over 200 automated tests pass.
  • Daily macro series from FRED.
In progress
  • Daily realized volatility from intraday trades: code and a 20-firm pilot; the full pull has not run yet.
  • A covariance network between firms: planned.
  • Wikipedia attention data: an 18-firm sample so far.
  • Firm IDs before 2016: those exits are listed but not resolved yet.

The aim: test whether a firm's attention or its place in the network leads its realized volatility, and how much dropping delisted firms distorts a backtest.

Firm IDs: an LLM proposes, two sources check

How one ID is decided
  • A local LLM names the company that traded under the ticker on its last day in the index. That is its only job.
  • SEC EDGAR supplies the ID. The CIK is the one EDGAR's records give for that name, never a number the model wrote.
  • Wikipedia is the second witness. Its record of index changes says which firm S&P removed that day. When it disagrees with the model, the model's answer is dropped and Wikipedia's name is searched instead.
  • Conflicts are withheld, not guessed. When the evidence disagrees, the ID stays empty and the reason is recorded.
Why the checks matter

The model was also asked for each CIK. None of its 273 was right, and it rated every one "high" confidence. It fits names to letters: TAP became Atmos Energy (it is Molson Coors).

An early version trusted an EDGAR name match alone. An audit found that 44 of its 144 IDs (31%) were the wrong firm, real companies whose names happen to fit the ticker. With the second source and rules for known traps, the current build finds 212 of the 273 IDs and withholds 61.

Limits

  • Firm IDs start in 2016. Exits before 2016 are listed but unresolved, and 57 segments from 2016 on still have no ID.
  • Known mismatches being fixed. A few current members carry a successor company's ID for years before that company existed, and two firms are missing from 2016 on.
  • The hand check is small and was not blind. On a 30-stay random sample, 24 IDs agreed, none was wrong and 6 were blank. The answers were drafted by an AI assistant after seeing the pipeline's output and reviewed by a team member. With 30 rows, these are estimates.
  • Licensed data stays licensed. The membership and firm-ID layers use only public sources; nothing from the licensed intraday data is published.
